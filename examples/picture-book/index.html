For the grown-up reading this aloud

This is a picture book about a tool that turns words into a book and into a film of someone reading it. Read one spread a night. The picture does most of the work, so let the child find things in it before you turn the page.

There is a machine on page five with dials on the front. It works. Let the child turn them and press Run.

Nothing in this book is magic, which is the point of it. A helper can only make what someone wrote down, and the order of the work never changes. A grown-up who has ever stayed up doing something a machine could have done will recognise the feeling.

This book was made the same way it describes. Appendix A shows the two files it came from.

I

The Bench at Night

Mara writes all day. The making happens at night.

Mara writes small things.

A gate. A lamp. A bird that sits on the gate and will not move for anything.

She writes them in a markdown file, which is a page of plain words with a # at the start of a title. Then she puts another page on top, and another, until the bench is a small hill of paper.

The writing is the part she knows. The making is the part that takes all night: a cover, a contents, eleven pages with a picture on each, a voice to read them aloud, and one long reel to hold the voice and the pictures together.

She never finishes before morning. So she stops writing, and she leaves a note.

II

The Note on the Bench

The first thing a helper reads is never the words.

The note sits on top of the pile. It is small, and it is the first thing anyone reads.

The Book of Small Things. Cover: a bird, a gate, a lamp. For Ada, who is five.

That is all of it. A name, a picture, and who it is for.

Mara does not write the note for herself. She knows what she meant. She writes it for whoever comes at night, because no one can make a book out of a pile, not even a helper. A helper can only make what someone wrote down.

III

Something Comes Up the Leg

Two small ones, and a lantern.

At midnight the bench leg gets visitors.

Tick comes first, blue and quick, with the lantern held high. Tock follows, smaller and slower, carrying nothing at all.

They climb onto the bench and they read the note. They read it before they touch a single page, every night, in the same order.

Tick, to Tock: "A bird. A gate. A lamp."

Tock, to Tick: "For Ada, who is five."

Then they begin.

IV

What They Make by Morning

One pile of pages. Two things to hold.

By morning there are two things on the bench.

The first is a book. A cover, a contents, eleven chapters numbered in a straight line, a picture beside the words on every page, and type set small enough to hold and large enough to read in bed.

The second is a reel. The same eleven pages, held on a screen one at a time, while a voice reads them out loud and leaves a small silence at the end of each one.

One pile of pages. Two things to hold.

Neither one arrives in a single flash. Each is a row of small steps, done in order, all night long.

V

Three Dials

The whole machine, on the front.

The machine has three dials.

The first says how many pages. The second says how fast the voice reads. The third says whose voice it is.

That is everything there is to set. There is no fourth dial, and nothing hidden behind the machine.

Turn them. Nothing breaks, and nothing is spent. A run that comes out wrong is only a run you can do again.

This panel is a simulation, not a renderer. Its time is an estimate for imaginary pages of 35 words each, not the length of this book’s narrated reel; the voice selector changes the description, not any audio.

VI

Too Fast

Tock turns one dial a little too far.

One night Tock turned the pace dial too far.

1.9x. The voice ran the words together like a river after rain. The pages flew past, a gate, a bird, a lamp, gone, gone, gone, and the silence at the end of each page was gone with them.

Ada watched, and laughed, and then stopped laughing, because she could not follow it. A picture you cannot reach is not a picture.

Tick put the dial back.

VII

Too Slow

Tock learns that the other way is a direction too.

The next night Tock was careful, and turned the same dial the other way.

0.3x. One page stayed on the screen so long that a spider built a web across the corner of it. The plant in the pot grew a leaf. Ada went to bed, came back after breakfast, and the same page was still there, halfway through being read.

Tock is patient. Tock is the most patient thing on the bench. Even Tock began to tap his foot.

VIII

Just Right

The one setting the whole book is about.

1.0x. The voice reads at the speed of a person reading to a child, and then it stops for one beat at the end of the page.

That beat is the whole thing. It is the space where a child looks at the picture. It is where a page is allowed to land.

The reel then takes as long as it takes. Nobody counts the minutes after the first two nights.

IX

The Rules the Elves Keep

Four rules, and they are not about software.

  1. One idea a page. A page that says two things says neither of them. The elves cut before they draw.
  2. The picture shows what the words cannot. A gate is a word. A bird that will not move is a picture.
  3. The words say it out loud. If a page only makes sense when you can see it, the reel is broken, and the elves know it before the reel does.
  4. Nothing is made that was not written down. The note comes first. There is no version of the story where the helpers invent the cover.

Tick and Tock have kept these rules for a long time, and they have never once been asked to break one.

X

Mara Stays Awake

One night she watches the order instead of sleeping through it.

One night Mara did not go to bed. She sat in the dark with a blanket and watched.

She watched them read the note. She watched them cut the pile into pages, and hold each page up to the lantern before they set it down. She watched them hand the pages to the voice, one at a time, and wait for each one to come back.

Then she watched the long part: frame after frame, held still, stitched into a single reel, the way eleven pages are stitched into a single book.

It took all night. It was the most interesting thing she had seen all year, because it was not magic. It was an order.

XI

The Last Small Note

The helper teaches the trade, and then goes.

The next night Mara stayed at the bench.

She wrote the note before she yawned. She turned the dials herself. She pressed Run, and she went to sleep while it worked.

In the morning there was a small folded note on the bench that she had not written.

You know the order now.

She kept it in the drawer with the good pencils.

∴

Things to Make Next

Three small things a reader can make tonight.

A one-page book. Write one page about one thing in your room. Give it a title, a cover, and a name on the front. That is a book. It takes ten minutes.

A reel of your own page. Read your page aloud, slowly, and stop for one beat at the end. Somebody will look at the picture in that beat. That somebody is the whole audience.

A note for a helper. Write down what you want made, for whom, and what the cover should be. Then hand it to anything that makes things for you, human or otherwise, and see how much better it goes.

Glossary

  • Markdown: a page of plain words. # makes a title, a blank line makes a new paragraph.
  • Run: one turn of the machine, from note to finished thing.
  • Pace: how fast the voice reads. Too fast and the pictures blur; too slow and a spider moves in.
  • Stitch: the last step, where many still frames become one reel.
